### What does GBIF do?

GBIF makes biodiversity information available through a shared international infrastructure. It brings together datasets from participating countries, research institutions, museums, government bodies, conservation organisations and other publishers.

Its core resources include species occurrence records, species and taxonomic information, geographical records, collection and observation data, dataset discovery, and data downloads and APIs.

**Important note:** GBIF does not independently generate every observation in its index. It aggregates and standardises information supplied by data publishers, and its systems interpret and index those records to make them searchable.

**The lowdown:** It’s particularly useful when an investigation needs to establish whether a species has been recorded in a particular area, identify historical observations, explore the distribution of organisms, or find datasets that may support environmental research.

### Use in Reporting

GBIF can be used to:

* Identify records of a species reported in a particular location or region.
* Establish a starting point for investigating historical species observations.
* Examine the distribution of recorded species around a site, facility or area of interest.
* Find biodiversity data relevant to environmental impact assessments and habitat investigations.
* Compare occurrence records across different periods, geographic areas and data publishers.
* Identify potential leads concerning wildlife presence, protected species or environmental change.
* Support research into changes in species distribution, biodiversity loss and ecosystem pressures.
* Locate original datasets and institutions that may provide additional evidence.
* Corroborate or challenge claims about where a species has been recorded.
* Produce reproducible analyses using downloaded data and persistent dataset citations.

As an example of real-world use, researchers compared biodiversity records from GBIF and iNaturalist with the data used in the environmental impact assessment for the [Tren Maya railway project](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14615517.2025.2534281). They identified underrepresentation of protected species and taxonomic diversity in the assessment.&#x20;

### Summary

GBIF is an international, open-access biodiversity data infrastructure that enables users to search, inspect, and download species occurrence records from a wide range of data publishers.

For OSINT, it’s most useful for collection and acquisition, enabling researchers to locate species records, examine geographical distribution patterns, and generate leads for environmental and wildlife-related investigations. As always, significant findings should be checked against original datasets, scientific or government sources and independent field evidence.

### Ownership

GBIF is an international network established in 2001 through a memorandum of understanding among participating governments.

It operates as an intergovernmental collaboration and research infrastructure rather than a privately owned commercial database. Its Secretariat is based in Copenhagen, Denmark, and coordinates the network's central services, software, data infrastructure and programme of work.

You can [find out more about its structure here](https://www.gbif.org/governance).

### Ethical Considerations

* Do not treat absence of records as proof of absence. Limited survey effort or incomplete reporting can explain why a species is not represented in the database.
* Check coordinate precision. Some records have approximate, rounded or obscured coordinates and may not support precise site-level conclusions.
* Protect sensitive species. Publicly available locations of threatened or commercially valuable organisms may create risks of disturbance, collection or poaching. Consider whether exact coordinates should be reproduced in a report.
* Respect data licences. Check the licence associated with the particular dataset and provide attribution as required.
* Historical records should not be presented as proof that a species is currently present.
* Be mindful that whilst an occurrence record can inform an environmental or wildlife investigation, it does not independently prove illegal activity, ecological harm, or regulatory non-compliance.
